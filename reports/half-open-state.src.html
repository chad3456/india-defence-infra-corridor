Mass mobilisation and state breakdown · a working paper

The Half-Open State

Protest does not break countries. Half-built institutions do. Across 176 countries measured today, the states tearing themselves apart are not the ones with the most activism — they are the ones with just enough freedom to organise and not enough state to absorb it.

Note on what was asked for

There is no Useful Idiot Index in this paper

This was commissioned as a country ranking: a score for how far protest and activism have captured each state. That score is not here, and it is worth being precise about why, because the reason is not squeamishness.

A measurement needs three things. A definition somebody else published, so it means the same thing next year. A dataset behind it, so it can be recomputed. And something external to check it against, so a wrong one can be caught. A useful-idiot score has none of the three. It would be an assertion wearing a decimal point, and it would be pointed at named countries and named movements — which is a serious accusation to generate from an algorithm invented for the purpose.

The question underneath it is real, researchable, and has a large empirical literature that mostly disagrees with the premise. So that is what this paper does: it asks when mass mobilisation is followed by state breakdown and when it is followed by reform, it shows the cases that cut each way, and it ends with where every country actually stands on things that are measured rather than on a score invented here.

One finding does support the intuition behind the request, and it is the paper's title. There is a danger zone. It is not the countries with the most activism. It is the ones stuck halfway.

Exhibit 1 · What happens after a campaign

The best-known dataset points the other way

Erica Chenoweth and Maria Stephan assembled every maximalist campaign between 1900 and 2006 that tried to overthrow a government or win territorial independence — 323 of them — and coded whether each was primarily violent or nonviolent, and what followed. It is the standard dataset in the field, and its results are not what the collapse thesis predicts.

Nonviolent campaigns succeeded roughly twice as often. Countries that went through one were more than eight times as likely to be a democracy five years later, and substantially less likely to fall back into civil war. On this evidence, mass mobilisation is not what breaks a state — armed insurgency is, and the nonviolent route is the one that tends to leave a functioning country behind.

The honest complication is that this is getting worse, not better. Chenoweth's later work finds the success rate of nonviolent campaigns fell from about 65% in the 1990s to under 34% since 2010, while peak participation dropped from around 2% of the population to 1.3%. Governments learned. They now blame foreigners, cut the internet, keep journalists out, and share suppression techniques with each other. That decline is real and it is a genuine mark against the optimistic reading of Exhibit 1.

Exhibit 2 · Computed from the data below

The danger is in the middle

In 2010 Jack Goldstone and colleagues published a forecasting model for the US Political Instability Task Force. Using onsets between 1955 and 2003, it distinguished countries that fell into instability from those that did not, two years ahead, at over 80% accuracy. Its strongest single predictor was not poverty, ethnic division or protest. It was regime type — specifically partial democracy with factionalism.

That model is fifteen years old. Here it is again, recomputed from scratch on the current data: every country sorted by how open it is, and the share of each band currently in an internal armed conflict.

Both curves are humped, and both peak in the middle. The most closed states are not the most violent. The most open states are the least violent by a wide margin — among the 75 countries with the freest association, 9% are at war with themselves; among the 15 stuck in the second band, 73% are.

This is the finding that supports the intuition behind the original request, and it is worth stating in its strongest form: a country with real freedom to organise and no institutions to route it through is the most dangerous configuration on the board. But note what it is not. The peak is not at the open end. Getting more open is not what moves a country into the danger zone — it is what moves a country out of it.

What actually predicts a country tearing itself apart

In 2003 James Fearon and David Laitin tested the assumption that civil wars come from grievance and ethnic division. They found they do not. What predicts civil war onset is poverty, weak state administration, rough terrain and large population — conditions that make insurgency practical. After controlling for income, ethnically diverse countries were no more likely to see civil violence than homogeneous ones.

Their result reproduces on current data. Each bar is the correlation between one measure and the log of conflict deaths per million people, across the countries that report both.

Income is the strongest predictor, then state capacity, then democracy. Freedom of association — the room a country gives to exactly the activity the collapse thesis blames — is the weakest of the four, and its sign is still negative. More room to organise goes with fewer people dying in internal wars, not more.

The case record

Both sides of the ledger, same treatment

Averages hide the cases. Mass mobilisation genuinely has been followed by state collapse, and any honest account has to carry those alongside the ones where it was followed by a functioning democracy. The pattern in the left column is not that the protests were unusually large or unusually foreign. It is what the state did next. In Syria, police in Daraa arrested about fifteen schoolboys in March 2011 for graffiti; four unarmed protesters were shot dead on 18 March; on 25 April the army began a ten-day siege of the city with tanks, helicopters and some 6,000 troops, killing more than 240 people. Peaceful protest became armed insurgency by September. Every step of that escalation was a decision taken by the state.

The right column had mobilisation too — often much larger. Poland's Solidarity ran for nine years. What differed was that the state, eventually, negotiated. The variable that separates the columns is not the presence or size of the movement. It is whether there were institutions capable of absorbing it, which is precisely what Exhibit 2 shows.

Weimar Germany is the case most often cited for the collapse thesis, and it belongs in the record with a correction. The violence that helped kill the republic was not civic protest: it was organised party paramilitaries, the SA and the Rotfrontkämpferbund, fighting each other with firearms. At least 105 people were killed in Prussia alone during the June–July 1932 election campaign, 18 of them on one day in Altona. That is armed factional militia, which the literature treats as a different phenomenon from mass mobilisation — and the distinction is doing real work, not being pedantic.

The measurable core of the idea

Foreign manipulation is real, documented, and counted

The "useful idiot" concept does point at something that exists. States really do manufacture and steer protest abroad, and this part is not speculative — it is catalogued. The Oxford Internet Institute's annual inventory found organised social media manipulation by political actors in 81 countries in its 2020 report, up from 70 the year before.

The clearest single documented case is small and specific. On 21 May 2016, a Facebook page called "Heart of Texas" with over 250,000 followers advertised a "Stop Islamization of Texas" rally outside the Islamic Da'wah Center in Houston. A second page, "United Muslims of America," with over 325,000 followers, advertised a counter-demonstration at the same address at the same hour. Both pages were run by the Internet Research Agency in St Petersburg. Real Americans on both sides turned up and confronted each other, covered live by local news. The US Senate Intelligence Committee laid the sequence out in its 2019 report.

That is the mechanism, demonstrated. Here is what it does not demonstrate. Nobody has shown that foreign-amplified protest is what causes states to fail — the Houston rally produced a shouting match, not a collapse. There is no dataset that codes movements by degree of foreign backing, which means the step from "influence operations exist" to "this movement is one" is currently unfalsifiable, and unfalsifiable accusations are exactly what governments in the bottom bands of Exhibit 2 use to justify shutting down domestic opposition. Chenoweth's own explanation for the falling success rate lists blaming foreigners as one of the techniques regimes learned.

Limits

What this paper cannot tell you

Exhibits 2 and 3 are cross-sectional. They are a snapshot of where countries sit now, not a study of what happened to them over time. Reverse causation is not a technicality here, it is probably the dominant effect: civil war destroys democracy and state capacity, so some of the correlation runs backwards from the direction the argument wants. The band counts are also small — 15 countries in the peak association band — and one or two reclassifications would move that column noticeably.

The democracy indices are expert-coded judgements, not counts of anything. V-Dem aggregates thousands of country-expert ratings. They are the best instrument available and they are contested, including for India, which V-Dem scores at 0.38 today against 0.74 in 2000 — a downgrade the Indian government rejects. The table shows V-Dem's number because it is V-Dem's table, not because the question is settled.

There is no protest-volume variable in here. The Mass Mobilization Project codes about 17,000 protest events across 162 countries and would be the right instrument; it stops in 2020 and is not published in a form this page could read. Everything above uses civic space as a proxy for the room protest has, which is a different thing from how much protest actually happens.

And the case record is selected. Sixteen countries were chosen before the data was pulled, from the cases the argument is about, so that the selection could not be tuned afterwards to make a line look better. It is still sixteen out of roughly two hundred.
